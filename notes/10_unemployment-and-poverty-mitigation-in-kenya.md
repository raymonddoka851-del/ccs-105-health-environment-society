TOPIC 10: UNEMPLOYMENT AND POVERTY MITIGATION IN KENYA

UNEMPLOYMENT

Unemployment remains one of the major socio-economic challenges facing many developing countries, including Kenya. The youth, who make up over 60% of Kenya’s population, are the most affected. However, they also represent the greatest potential resource for addressing the unemployment crisis through creativity, innovation, and entrepreneurship.

Expected learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

Explain the meaning and causes of unemployment among youth.

Discuss strategies for promoting youth participation in job creation.

Appreciate the importance of skills development and innovation in reducing unemployment.

Definition: Unemployment refers to the situation where people who are willing and able to work cannot find jobs.

There are several types of unemployment, each with different causes and characteristics. The main types are discussed below:

1. Frictional Unemployment

This occurs when people are temporarily between jobs or are entering the workforce for the first time.

It’s often short-term and happens because of the time it takes to find a suitable job.

Example: A recent college graduate looking for their first job or someone who quit their job to find a better one.

2. Structural Unemployment

Happens when there is a mismatch between the skills workers have and the skills needed by employers.

Often caused by technological changes, changes in consumer demand, or shifts in the economy.

Can be long-term because workers may need retraining.

Example: Factory workers losing jobs because of automation.

3. Cyclical Unemployment

Caused by economic downturns or recessions.

When demand for goods and services decreases, businesses reduce production and lay off workers.

It fluctuates with the business cycle.

Example: Layoffs during a recession when companies face reduced sales.

4. Seasonal Unemployment

Occurs when people are unemployed at certain times of the year because their jobs are seasonal.

Common in industries like agriculture, tourism, and retail.

Example: Farm workers unemployed after harvest season or holiday retail workers after the season ends.

5. Classical (or Real-Wage) Unemployment

Happens when wages are kept above the market-clearing level, causing excess labor supply.

Often due to minimum wage laws, labor unions, or other wage rigidities.

Example: Workers willing to work for lower wages can’t find jobs because wages are fixed too high.

main causes of unemployment explained clearly:

1. Economic Downturns (Recessions)

When the economy slows down, businesses sell fewer goods and services.

To cut costs, companies lay off workers, causing cyclical unemployment.

Example: During a recession, factories reduce production and workers lose jobs.

2. Technological Changes

Automation and new technologies can replace human labor.

Workers whose skills become outdated may lose their jobs, leading to structural unemployment.

Example: Assembly line workers replaced by robots.

3. Mismatch of Skills

Sometimes the skills workers have don’t match the skills employers need.

This happens when industries change or new industries emerge.

Result: People remain unemployed because they need retraining or education.

Example: Coal miners losing jobs as the economy shifts to renewable energy.

4. Seasonal Changes

Certain industries only need workers during specific seasons.

Off-season, these workers are temporarily unemployed.

Example: Ski instructors or agricultural workers during off-peak times.

5. Government Policies

Minimum wage laws, labor unions, and unemployment benefits can sometimes discourage hiring or reduce the incentive to work.

Example: If minimum wages are set too high, some employers may hire fewer workers.

6. Lack of Job Creation

If the economy isn’t growing enough, there won’t be enough new jobs for people entering the workforce.

This causes higher unemployment, especially for young people.

7. Population Growth

Rapid growth in the working-age population can increase unemployment if job creation doesn’t keep up.

More people are competing for the same number of jobs.

8. Globalization

Jobs may move to countries with cheaper labor, causing unemployment in some industries in other countries.

Example: Manufacturing jobs moving from developed to developing countries.

effective ways to reduce unemployment:

1. Promoting Economic Growth

When the economy grows, businesses expand and create more jobs.

Governments can boost growth through investments in infrastructure, supporting industries, and encouraging entrepreneurship.

2. Improving Education and Training

Providing education and vocational training helps workers gain the skills employers need.

This reduces structural unemployment by matching workers’ skills to job market demands.

3. Encouraging Labor Market Flexibility

Making it easier for businesses to hire and fire workers can reduce classical unemployment.

This can include reforming labor laws or reducing excessive regulations.

4. Supporting Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs)

SMEs are often major job creators.

Governments can provide financial support, reduce taxes, or simplify business registration to help SMEs grow and hire more people.

5. Implementing Active Labor Market Policies

Programs like job counseling, job matching services, and subsidies for hiring the unemployed can help people find jobs faster.

Example: Public job fairs or online job portals.

6. Encouraging Technological Adaptation and Retraining

Helping workers adapt to new technologies through retraining programs reduces structural unemployment.

Governments and businesses can partner to offer these training opportunities.

7. Reducing Seasonal Unemployment

Diversifying the economy so there are year-round job opportunities in more sectors.

Encouraging tourism or agriculture innovations that extend the working season.

8. Monetary and Fiscal Policies

Central banks can lower interest rates to encourage borrowing and investment.

Governments can increase spending or cut taxes to stimulate demand and create jobs, especially during recessions.

9. Encouraging Entrepreneurship

Supporting startups and self-employment can generate new jobs.

This can be done through training, grants, and easing access to credit.

Role of Youth in Addressing Unemployment

Introduction

3. Main Content

a) Entrepreneurship and Innovation

Youth can become job creators rather than job seekers by starting small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs).

Innovation and technology (e.g., digital startups, e-commerce, agritech) can generate new forms of employment.

Government initiatives like the Ajira Digital Program and Hustler Fund and nyota fund encourage youth entrepreneurship.

b) Skills Development and Education

Youth should actively pursue vocational and technical training through TVET institutions.

Continuous upskilling and reskilling ensures they remain relevant to emerging industries (ICT, renewable energy, data analysis).

Partnerships between schools, industries, and government can bridge the skills gap.

c) Community and Cooperative Development

Youth can engage in community-based projects such as agriculture, waste management, and eco-tourism.

Formation of youth cooperatives enables collective investment and employment creation at local levels.

d) Advocacy and Policy Participation

Youth should participate in policy dialogues and national development forums.

Through youth councils, NGOs, and online platforms, they can advocate for:

Fair employment policies.

Youth-inclusive budgeting.

Internship and apprenticeship programs.

e) Digital Economy Participation

The digital sector offers vast opportunities for online work, content creation, freelancing, and digital marketing.

Youth can leverage platforms such as Upwork, Fiverr, and Ajira Digital to earn income remotely.

ICT skills open access to global employment opportunities.

f) Promoting Sustainable and Green Jobs

Youth can engage in climate-smart agriculture, renewable energy, and environmental conservation.

These areas provide employment while contributing to sustainable development.

g) Networking, Mentorship, and Volunteering

Youth should build professional networks and join mentorship programs to gain exposure.

Volunteering enhances experience, discipline, and employability.

Collaboration with private sector and NGOs can expand access to job markets.

Challenges Facing Youth in Addressing Unemployment

Limited access to finance and credit.

Inadequate entrepreneurial skills.

Mismatch between education and labor market needs.

Lack of mentorship and exposure.

Policy implementation gaps.

5. Strategies to Strengthen Youth Involvement

Expand funding for youth enterprises.

Support vocational and digital skills programs.

Encourage innovation hubs and incubation centers.

Create enabling environments for startups and informal businesses.

Strengthen partnerships between government, private sector, and educational institutions.

Conclusion

The youth are a vital force in combating unemployment through their energy, creativity, and innovation.

When empowered with skills, resources, and opportunities, they can drive economic growth, job creation, and social transformation. Sustainable solutions to unemployment must therefore place youth at the center of national development strategies.

POVERTY

Introduction:

Poverty is a complex and multidimensional social, economic, and political phenomenon. It refers to a condition in which individuals or groups lack the financial resources and essentials for a minimum standard of living. Poverty affects health, education, access to basic services, and participation in society. Understanding poverty helps in formulating policies that promote social justice, economic equality, and sustainable development.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students should be able to:

Define and explain the concept of poverty.

Identify the different types and dimensions of poverty.

Understand the causes and effects of poverty.

Examine strategies for poverty reduction and eradication.

Discuss global and national initiatives aimed at poverty alleviation.

What is Poverty?

Poverty is generally defined as a state of deprivation where people lack access to basic human needs such as food, clean water, shelter, healthcare, and education.

Key Definitions:

World Bank (2022): Poverty is living on less than US$2.15 per day (international poverty line).

UN (2015): Poverty is the denial of choices and opportunities, a violation of human dignity. It means lack of basic capacity to participate effectively in society.

Absolute Poverty: When individuals cannot meet the minimum level of income required for basic living.

Relative Poverty: When individuals’ income or resources are significantly less than the average within a society, leading to social exclusion.

Types of Poverty

Absolute Poverty: Lack of basic necessities such as food, water, shelter, and healthcare.

Relative Poverty: Exists when individuals have less income compared to others in the same society.

Situational Poverty: Caused by a sudden crisis or loss (e.g., natural disasters, job loss).

Chronic Poverty: Persistent poverty that continues over generations.

Urban Poverty: Found in metropolitan areas, often linked to slum living conditions.

Rural Poverty: Associated with lack of infrastructure, employment opportunities, and access to services in rural areas.

Multidimensional Poverty: Includes not only income but also lack of education, health, and living standards.

Causes of Poverty

Poverty results from multiple, interconnected factors including:

Economic Causes:

Unemployment and underemployment

Low wages and unequal distribution of income

Economic recessions and inflation

Social Causes:

Poor access to education and healthcare

Gender inequality

Discrimination based on ethnicity, race, or class

Political Causes:

Corruption and poor governance

Political instability and conflict

Lack of inclusive economic policies

Environmental Causes:

Droughts, floods, and environmental degradation

Climate change impacts on agriculture

Structural Causes:

Unequal land ownership

Historical injustices (e.g., colonial legacies)

Weak institutional frameworks

Effects of Poverty

Health: Malnutrition, disease, and limited access to healthcare.

Education: Low enrollment and high dropout rates.

Economic: Low productivity and limited economic growth.

Social: Crime, insecurity, and social exclusion.

Political: Disenfranchisement and political instability.

Environmental: Overexploitation of natural resources due to survival needs.

Measurement of Poverty

Common tools used to measure poverty include:

Poverty Line: The minimum income required to meet basic needs.

Headcount Index: Percentage of the population living below the poverty line.

Poverty Gap Index: Measures the intensity of poverty.

Human Development Index (HDI): Composite measure including income, education, and health.

Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI): Developed by UNDP to assess multiple deprivations at once.

Strategies for Poverty Reduction

Economic Empowerment: Job creation, entrepreneurship, and microfinance.

Education and Skills Training: Improving literacy and vocational skills.

Healthcare Access: Ensuring affordable healthcare services.

Social Protection Programs: Cash transfers, pensions, and food subsidies.

Agricultural Development: Promoting sustainable farming and access to markets.

Good Governance: Transparency and equitable resource distribution.

Infrastructure Development: Improving transport, energy, and communication systems.

Environmental Sustainability: Supporting climate-resilient livelihoods.

Global and National Efforts

United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 1 (SDG 1): End poverty in all its forms everywhere by 2030.

World Bank Poverty Reduction Strategy Papers (PRSPs): National frameworks for poverty reduction.

Kenya Vision 2030: Aims to transform Kenya into a middle-income country with a high quality of life.

National Social Protection Policy (Kenya): Supports vulnerable populations through cash transfer programs.

Conclusion

Poverty is not merely a lack of income but a multidimensional issue involving social, economic, political, and environmental factors. Addressing poverty requires comprehensive and inclusive strategies focusing on equity, empowerment, and sustainable development.
