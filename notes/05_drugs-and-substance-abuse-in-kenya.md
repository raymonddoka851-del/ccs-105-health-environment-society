5. Educational Consequences Among students and young people, substance misuse can interfere with education and personal development. Possible consequences include: Poor concentration and memory Declining academic performance Absenteeism and lateness Loss of interest in studies Disciplinary problems Dropping out of school or college Reduced participation in extracurricular activities Difficulty completing assignments and examinations Early substance use can therefore interfere with educational opportunities and future career development.

4. Social and Family Consequences Addiction rarely affects only the individual. Families and social relationships can also suffer. Frequent arguments and conflict within the family Loss of trust Social isolation Separation or divorce Neglect of children or other dependants Domestic violence in some cases Loss of friendships and social support Financial dependence on family members Emotional stress among parents, partners, siblings, and children Family members may experience fear, shame, anxiety, anger, helplessness, or financial hardship.

--- slide 36 ---

7. Legal and Criminal Consequences Substance misuse can increase exposure to legal problems, although substance use itself is treated differently under the laws of different countries. Possible consequences include: Arrest or prosecution for possession or trafficking where prohibited Driving under the influence Disorderly or violent behavior Theft or other crimes committed to obtain money for substances Legal expenses and fines Imprisonment A criminal record that may affect employment and education It is important to distinguish between substance use , which is a health issue, and criminal behavior associated with or surrounding substance use.

--- slide 36 ---

6. Occupational and Economic Consequences Addiction can affect an individual's ability to obtain and maintain employment. Consequences may include: Reduced productivity Absenteeism Accidents and injuries at work Poor relationships with colleagues or supervisors Job loss Difficulty finding future employment Reduced income Spending large amounts of money on substances Debt and financial instability Dependence on relatives or social services The economic consequences may extend to families when household income is diverted toward substance use rather than food, housing, education, or healthcare.

--- slide 36 ---

3. Cognitive and Behavioral Consequences Substance abuse can interfere with judgment, concentration, memory, decision-making, and self-control. A person may: Make impulsive or risky decisions. Have difficulty concentrating or remembering information. Become increasingly preoccupied with obtaining and using the substance. Neglect responsibilities and personal hygiene. Display aggression, irritability, or unpredictable behavior. Engage in dangerous activities, including driving while impaired. Experience withdrawal symptoms when they stop or reduce use. Over time, obtaining and using the substance may become a central priority, replacing previously important activities and relationships.

18. Previous substance use The use of one substance can sometimes increase exposure to other substances. For example, someone who regularly drinks alcohol or uses tobacco may encounter social environments where other drugs are available. This is sometimes discussed as polysubstance use , meaning the use of multiple substances, either at the same time or during the same period.

--- slide 36 ---

17. Cultural and community factors Attitudes toward alcohol and other substances differ between communities. Risk can increase where: Heavy drinking is socially accepted. Drug use is common within a community. There are few prevention programs. Treatment services are difficult to access. Substance use is associated with particular social activities. Conversely, strong community support and clear social norms against harmful substance use can provide protection.

--- slide 36 ---

2. Psychological and Mental Health Consequences Addiction can substantially affect emotional and psychological well-being. Anxiety, irritability, and agitation Depression and emotional instability Panic attacks Paranoia and suspiciousness Hallucinations or psychotic symptoms with some substances Sleep disturbances Reduced ability to cope with stress Dependence on substances to feel normal or function Worsening of pre-existing mental health problems There can also be a harmful cycle in which substance use temporarily relieves distress but ultimately worsens mental health, leading to further substance use.

Physical/Health Consequences Substance abuse can damage almost every major organ system, depending on the substance, dose, duration, and route of use. Brain and nervous system: impaired coordination, memory problems, seizures, changes in brain function, and neurological damage. Heart and blood vessels: abnormal heart rhythms, high or low blood pressure, heart attack, stroke, and other cardiovascular problems. Liver: particularly with alcohol and some drugs, prolonged use can cause fatty liver, hepatitis, cirrhosis, and liver failure. Lungs: smoking or inhaling substances can cause chronic respiratory problems, lung infections, and other pulmonary damage. Kidneys: some substances can contribute to kidney injury or failure. Immune system: substance misuse may weaken health and increase susceptibility to infections. Nutrition: addiction may lead to poor appetite, inadequate nutrition, weight loss, or vitamin deficiencies. Overdose: excessive consumption can cause respiratory depression, unconsciousness, coma, permanent organ damage, or death.

Strategies to Address Drug and Substance Abuse in Kenya (a) Government strategies NACADA campaigns to educate the public. Ban on second-generation and illicit brews. Laws restricting alcohol sales near schools and regulating drinking hours. Community policing and crackdowns on drug trafficking.

Legal Framework on Drug and Substance Abuse The alarming prevalence of drug abuse in the country and among the youth in particular has continued despite the existing legislation and its enforcement by the authorities. The government made legislations to deal with drugs, to control access of drugs to certain categories of people and rightly outlaw the use of others, some of the laws in the statute books that specifically are aimed at controlling drug use. The government enacted the liquor licensing Act in 1986 among others regulate the consumption of alcohol, the law prohibits bar to sell alcohol to school going children and under aged children. In 1994 the Narcotics Drugs and psychotropic substances control Act was enacted lagging heavy penalties on drug traffickers. The National Policy on Drug Abuse in Kenya was developed on the premise that the Kenya Government had ratified three major UN conventions on narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances , namely the Single Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1961 ; The Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1971 and the Convention against Illicit Trafficking in Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances of 1988.

12. Treatment, Recovery, and Relapse-Related Consequences Addiction is a treatable health condition, but recovery may involve significant challenges. Withdrawal can produce uncomfortable or, with some substances, medically dangerous symptoms. Treatment may require medical care, counseling, behavioral therapy, rehabilitation, or medication depending on the substance and individual circumstances. Relapse can occur and should generally be viewed as a signal that treatment needs to be reassessed rather than simply as a moral failure. Recovery may require rebuilding relationships, employment, education, finances, and social support.

9. Effects on Children and Future Generations Children living with parental substance addiction may experience significant difficulties. These may include: Neglect or inadequate supervision Emotional insecurity Poor school performance Exposure to conflict or violence Financial hardship Social stigma Increased risk of developing substance-use problems themselves Substance exposure during pregnancy can also affect fetal development, with consequences varying according to the substance and pattern of exposure.

--- slide 36 ---

8. Sexual and Reproductive Health Consequences Some substances can impair judgment and increase risky sexual behavior. Possible consequences include: Unprotected sexual intercourse Multiple or high-risk sexual partners Increased exposure to sexually transmitted infections, including HIV Sexual dysfunction Reduced fertility in some circumstances Pregnancy complications Poor prenatal care Harm to a developing fetus when certain substances are used during pregnancy Intoxication can also reduce a person's ability to make informed decisions or recognize unsafe situations.

--- slide 36 ---

11. Overdose, Disability, and Death One of the most serious consequences of substance misuse is overdose . An overdose can occur when a person takes more of a substance than the body can safely tolerate, or when substances interact dangerously. Depending on the substance, overdose may result in: Severe respiratory depression Loss of consciousness Seizures Cardiac complications Brain injury Coma Permanent disability Death

--- slide 36 ---

10. Community and Societal Consequences At the broader level, widespread substance misuse can place substantial pressure on communities and public services. These consequences can include: Increased healthcare costs Increased demand for addiction treatment and rehabilitation Workplace and road accidents Crime and violence associated with some patterns of substance use Homelessness and social instability Reduced workforce productivity Increased burden on law-enforcement and judicial systems Loss of life and disability Greater strain on families and social-support systems Thus, addiction should not be viewed solely as an individual problem; it can become a significant public-health and social problem .

--- slide 36 ---

5. Family influence The family environment can strongly influence substance-use behavior. Risk may increase when: Parents or caregivers misuse alcohol or drugs. There is frequent conflict within the family. Children receive little supervision or guidance. There is neglect or abuse. Family members normalize heavy drinking or drug use. Communication between parents and children is poor. A family history of substance-use problems can also contribute through both genetic vulnerability and learned behavior .

--- slide 36 ---

4. Mental health problems Mental health conditions can increase the risk of problematic substance use. These may include: Depression Anxiety Post-traumatic stress Bipolar disorder Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) Certain personality disorders Trauma-related problems Some individuals may use substances to reduce unpleasant symptoms, improve their mood, sleep, or confidence. This is sometimes called self-medication .

--- slide 36 ---

7. Availability and accessibility of drugs The easier substances are to obtain, the greater the opportunity for use. Availability can be influenced by: Illegal drug markets Alcohol outlets Tobacco availability Prescription-drug access Weak enforcement of age restrictions Drugs being readily available within a person's social network Easy access does not automatically cause addiction, but it can make initiation and continued use more likely.

--- slide 36 ---

6. Genetics and biological factors Genes can influence how vulnerable an individual is to developing a substance-use disorder. Some people may inherit characteristics that affect: How strongly they experience the rewarding effects of a drug. How their brain responds to stress. Impulse control and risk-taking. The likelihood of developing dependence. Genetics do not mean that someone is destined to become addicted. Rather, they can increase or decrease susceptibility when combined with environmental and behavioral factors.

--- slide 36 ---

3. Stress and emotional problems Some people use drugs as a way of coping with difficult emotions or stressful situations. Common sources of stress include: Financial difficulties Relationship problems Academic pressure Unemployment Family conflicts Bereavement Work-related stress Social discrimination Chronic stress A person may temporarily feel relaxed or emotionally numb after taking a substance. However, relying on drugs to manage stress can create a cycle in which the person increasingly depends on the substance.

--- slide 36 ---

Commonly Abused Drugs and Substances in Kenya Alcohol – the most widely abused substance, including illicit brews ( chang’aa , busaa ) and legal drinks. Tobacco – both cigarettes and smokeless forms. Cannabis (bhang/marijuana) – widely abused, especially among young people. Khat (miraa/ muguka ) – a legal stimulant crop but often abused, leading to addiction and health issues. Prescription drugs – misuse of painkillers (codeine, tramadol) and cough syrups. Hard drugs – heroin, cocaine, and methamphetamine, especially in coastal towns like Mombasa and urban centers like Nairobi. Inhalants – glue, petrol, and other solvents, mostly among street children.

2. Curiosity and experimentation Curiosity is an important reason why some people first try drugs. People may wonder: What does the substance feel like? Why do other people enjoy it? Will it make me feel more confident or energetic? What will happen if I try it just once? Although experimentation does not always lead to addiction, repeated use can increase the risk of developing problematic use.

--- slide 36 ---

Causes of Drug and Substances Abusive 1. Peer pressure and social influence People, particularly adolescents and young adults, may begin using drugs because their friends or social groups use them. Friends may encourage someone to experiment with alcohol, cannabis, tobacco, or other drugs. A person may use substances to feel accepted or avoid being excluded. Some social groups may portray drug use as fashionable, enjoyable, or a sign of maturity. Continued exposure to people who use drugs can make substance use seem normal. Example: A student may start smoking or drinking because classmates regularly do so and encourage them to participate.

--- slide 36 ---

14. Personality and behavioral factors Certain characteristics can increase vulnerability, although they do not directly cause addiction. Examples include: Impulsivity Sensation-seeking Difficulty controlling behavior Poor decision-making Low frustration tolerance Desire for immediate rewards Risk-taking behavior These characteristics may make experimentation or continued substance use more likely.

--- slide 36 ---

13. Easy access to prescription medicines Some people develop substance-use problems after using medications prescribed for legitimate medical reasons. For example, certain prescription medications can produce dependence when misused or taken for longer than medically appropriate. Risk can increase when medicines are: Taken in larger amounts than prescribed. Used more frequently than prescribed. Taken without a prescription. Shared with other people. Combined with other substances. Prescription medicines should therefore be used according to medical instructions.

--- slide 36 ---

16. Academic and workplace pressure Students may misuse substances because of: Examination pressure Fear of failure Competition Poor academic performance Desire to stay awake or concentrate Social pressure Adults may similarly use substances because of: Long working hours Job insecurity Workplace stress Performance pressure Difficult working conditions

--- slide 36 ---

15. Lack of positive recreational activities When people have limited access to healthy activities, they may have more opportunities to engage in risky behaviors. Protective activities can include: Sports Music Art Community organizations Religious or cultural activities Volunteering Employment and vocational training Clubs and youth programs Such activities can provide social connection, purpose, and healthier ways of coping with stress.

--- slide 36 ---

12. Media and social media influence Movies, music, advertisements, online content, and social media can sometimes portray substance use positively or as glamorous. Repeated exposure can contribute to perceptions that: Drinking heavily is normal. Drug use is exciting. Drugs make people more attractive or confident. Substance use is an acceptable way to deal with problems. Media influence is only one factor and does not affect everyone in the same way.

--- slide 36 ---

9. Trauma, abuse, and neglect People who experience traumatic events may have an increased risk of problematic substance use. Trauma can include: Physical abuse Sexual abuse Emotional abuse Domestic violence Childhood neglect Serious accidents War or displacement Loss of a loved one Other frightening or life-threatening experiences Drugs may be used to temporarily escape painful memories or emotions.

--- slide 36 ---

8. Poverty and unemployment Economic hardship can contribute indirectly to substance abuse. People experiencing poverty or unemployment may face: Chronic stress Hopelessness Social exclusion Poor living conditions Lack of opportunities Exposure to drug-using environments Some individuals may turn to substances as a way of coping with these circumstances. It is important to note that poverty itself does not cause drug abuse . People from all socioeconomic backgrounds can develop substance-use disorders.

--- slide 36 ---

11. Poor education and lack of awareness Some people may underestimate the risks associated with drugs. For example, they may believe: "I can stop whenever I want." "Using it once cannot hurt me." "Prescription drugs are always safe." "Everyone uses this substance." "Addiction only happens to certain types of people." Accurate education can help people understand the physical, psychological, social, and economic consequences of substance abuse.

--- slide 36 ---

10. Lack of parental supervision and guidance Young people who receive little supervision may have greater exposure to risky behaviors. Protective parenting generally includes: Knowing where children are and who they spend time with. Setting reasonable boundaries. Discussing drugs openly. Monitoring behavior appropriately. Providing emotional support. Establishing clear expectations. Poor supervision can make it easier for adolescents to become involved with drug-using peers.
