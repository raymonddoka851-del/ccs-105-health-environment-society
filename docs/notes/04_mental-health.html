Topic 4: MENTAL HEALTH

According to the World Health Organization (WHO)

“Mental health is a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn well and work well, and contribute to their community.”

The WHO states that mental health is “more than the absence of mental disorders.” Peak mental health is about managing active conditions and maintaining wellness and happiness.

The organization also emphasizes that preserving and restoring mental health is important at individual, community, and societal levels.

Factors contributing to mental health issues

Mental health conditions are complex and usually caused by a combination of biological, psychological, and social/lifestyle factors. No single cause explains all mental health issues — instead, different risk factors interact and increase the likelihood of developing a condition. The main contributing factors include:

  1. Biological Factors

Factor

How It Contributes

Genetics / Family history

Mental health conditions can run in families (e.g. depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia).

Brain chemistry

Imbalances in neurotransmitters like serotonin, dopamine, or norepinephrine can affect mood and behavior.

Hormonal changes

Hormonal imbalances (e.g. thyroid issues, postpartum changes) can impact mental health.

Prenatal exposure

Exposure to drugs, alcohol, infections, or stress during pregnancy can affect brain development.

Physical illness / injury

Chronic conditions or brain injuries (like concussions or tumors) can contribute to mental health problems.

Substance use

Alcohol and drugs can trigger or worsen mental health conditions, especially anxiety, psychosis, or mood disorders.

  1. Psychological Factors

Factor

How It Contributes

Childhood trauma or abuse

Early exposure to trauma (emotional, physical, or sexual) increases risk of many disorders.

Neglect or attachment issues

Lack of emotional support or insecure attachment in childhood affects emotional development.

Low self-esteem

Chronic self-criticism, shame, or feelings of worthlessness are linked to depression and anxiety.

Negative thinking patterns

Rigid or distorted thinking can fuel anxiety, depression, and other issues (e.g., catastrophizing).

Coping skills

Poor ability to manage stress, frustration, or emotions increases vulnerability to mental health issues.

  1. Social and Environmental Factors

Factor

How It Contributes

Stressful life events

Loss of a loved one, divorce, unemployment, or major illness can trigger mental health conditions.

Poverty or financial stress

Chronic financial insecurity is linked to higher rates of anxiety and depression.

Social isolation

Lack of supportive relationships increases risk of depression and emotional distress.

Discrimination or stigma

Racism, sexism, homophobia, or ableism can create chronic stress and lower self-worth.

Unstable or unsafe environment

Living in violent, chaotic, or unstable environments can contribute to mental illness.

Peer pressure or bullying

Especially in youth, these can lead to anxiety, self-harm, or suicidal thoughts.

  1. Lifestyle Factors

Factor

How It Contributes

Lack of sleep

Chronic sleep deprivation is strongly linked to depression, anxiety, and mood swings.

Poor diet

Nutritional deficiencies (e.g., B vitamins, omega-3) can affect brain function.

Lack of physical activity

Sedentary lifestyle increases risk of depression and cognitive decline.

Substance misuse

Recreational or prescription drug misuse can worsen or cause mental health symptoms.

Technology overuse

Excessive social media or screen time is linked to anxiety, low mood, and attention issues.

  1. Major Life Transitions or Developmental Factors

Puberty, aging, and menopause can bring psychological challenges.

Leaving home, starting a new job, or retiring can affect identity and stability.

Becoming a parent or caregiver may increase stress and emotional strain.

Risk Increases When Factors Combine: Often, mental health conditions arise when multiple risk factors overlap. For example: A person with a genetic predisposition to depression who also experiences childhood trauma, and is now under chronic financial stress, has a significantly higher risk of developing a depressive disorder.

✅ Protective Factors (That Help Prevent Mental Illness)

Strong family and social support

Healthy coping skills

Stable income and housing

Access to mental health care

Good sleep, diet, and exercise

Positive self-esteem and identity

Safe and supportive community environment

Types of mental health disorders

Healthcare professionals group mental health disorders according to the features they have in common. They include:

anxiety disorders

mood disorders

schizophrenia disorders

ANXIETY DISORDERS

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health disorders.

People with these conditions have severe fear or anxiety related to certain objects or situations. Most people with an anxiety disorder try to avoid exposure to whatever triggers their anxiety. Below are some examples of anxiety disorders.

i). Generalized anxiety disorder

Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) involves excessive worry or fear that disrupts everyday living.

People may also experience physical symptoms, including: restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, tense muscles and interrupted sleep

Anxiety symptoms do not always have a specific trigger in people with GAD.

They may experience excessive anxiety when encountering everyday situations that do not pose a direct danger, such as chores or appointments. A person with GAD may sometimes feel anxiety with no trigger at all.

ii). Panic disorder

People with panic disorder experience regular panic attacks involving sudden, overwhelming terror or a sense of imminent disaster and death.

iii). Phobias

There are many types of phobias, and what may seem typical to one person might be a severe problem that dominates daily life for another. Different types include:

Simple phobias: These may involve a disproportionate fear of specific objects, scenarios, or animals. A fear of spiders is a typical example.

Social phobia: Sometimes known as social anxiety, this is a fear of being watched or judged by others. People with social phobia often restrict their exposure to social environments.

Agoraphobia: The term “agoraphobia” refers to a fear of situations where getting away may be difficult, such as being in an elevator or a moving train. People sometimes misunderstand this phobia as a fear of being outside.

iv). Obsessive-compulsive disorder

People with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) may experience constant, stressful thoughts and an urge to perform repetitive acts, such as handwashing.

v). Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

PTSD can occur after a person experiences or witnesses a stressful or traumatic event. During this type of event, the person thinks their life or other people’s lives are in danger. They may feel afraid or that they have no control over what is happening. These sensations of trauma and fear may then contribute to PTSD.

What are traumatic events?

Examples of events that can trigger PTSD include accidents, wars, crimes, fires, the death of a loved one, abuse, or any event that causes shock and fear. Thoughts and memories may recur even though the danger has passed.

MOOD DISORDERS

These may also be referred to as affective disorders or depressive disorders.

People with these conditions have significant mood changes, generally involving either mania, a period of high energy and joy, or depression. Examples of mood disorders include:

Major depression: An individual with major depression experiences a constant low mood and loses interest in activities and events that they previously enjoyed (anhedonia). They may feel prolonged periods of sadness or extreme sadness.

Bipolar disorder: A person with bipolar disorder experiences unusual changes in their mood, energy levels, levels of activity, and ability to continue with daily life. Periods of high mood are known as manic phases, while depressive phases bring on low mood.

Seasonal affective disorder (SAD): Reduced daylight during the fall, winter, and early spring months triggers SAD, a type of major depression. It is most common in countries far from the equator.

Read more about the different types of bipolar disorder.

SCHIZOPHRENIA DISORDERS

The term “schizophrenia” often refers to a spectrum of disorders characterized by features of psychosis and other severe symptoms. These are highly complex conditions.

Signs of schizophrenia typically develop between the ages of 16 and 30. A person will have thoughts that appear fragmented, and may also find it hard to process information.

Schizophrenia has negative and positive symptoms. Positive symptoms include delusions, thought disorders, and hallucinations, while withdrawal, lack of motivation, and a flat or inappropriate mood are examples of negative symptoms.

Early signs of mental illness

No physical test or scan reliably indicates whether a person has developed a mental health condition. However, people should look out for the following as possible signs:

withdrawing from friends, family, and colleagues

avoiding activities, they would usually enjoy

sleeping too much or too little

eating too much or too little

feeling hopeless

having consistently low energy

using mood-altering substances, including alcohol and nicotine, more frequently

displaying negative emotions

being confused

being unable to complete daily tasks, such as getting to work or cooking a meal

having persistent thoughts or memories that reappear regularly

thinking of causing physical harm to themselves or others

hearing voices

experiencing delusions

Diagnosis

Diagnosing a mental health disorder requires a multistep process. A doctor may begin by looking at a person’s medical history and performing a thorough physical exam to rule out a physical condition that may be causing the symptoms. They may also order laboratory tests, such as imaging exams and blood tests, to screen for possible underlying causes.

The healthcare professional will also do a psychological evaluation. This includes asking about a person’s symptoms and experiences and how these affect the individual’s life. The doctor may ask a person to complete mental health questionnaires to help understand their thoughts, feelings, and behavior patterns.,

Treatment/management of mental health

Various methods are available to help manage mental health conditions. Treatment is highly individual, and what works for one person may not work for another.

Some strategies or treatments are more successful in combination with others. A person with a mental health disorder may choose different options at various stages in their life.

It is important for an individual to work closely with a doctor who can help them identify their needs and provide suitable treatment.

Psychotherapy, or talking therapies

The following types of therapy take a psychological approach to treating mental health:

i). Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)

Is a therapy technique that aims to help people find new ways to behave by changing their thought patterns. Therapists may use strategies such as role-playing and assigning homework.

How does it work?

Some forms of psychotherapy focus on looking into the past to understand current feelings. Instead, CBT focuses on present thoughts and beliefs. It emphasizes the need to identify, challenge, and change how a person views a situation.

Although counselors typically personalize sessions to an individual, CBT steps may include:

an individual assessment

establishing goals and areas to work on early in the process

regularly checking the person’s mood and any life updates

collaboratively setting agendas for each session

reviewing any homework

punctuating discussions with feedback and summaries

assigning further homework at the end of sessions

ii). Exposure therapy

Exposure therapy is a psychological treatment that helps individuals confront and reduce fear, anxiety, or distress related to specific stimuli or situations. It's primarily used to treat various anxiety disorders, but also has applications in PTSD, OCD, and other mental health conditions.

It is a type of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) that involves:

Gradually exposing a person to the feared object, thought, or situation.

Helping them face their fears rather than avoid them.

Reducing avoidance behaviors and distorted thinking that reinforce anxiety.

Conditions Is It Used For include:

Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

Exposure to trauma-related memories, images, or situations in a safe and controlled way.

Often includes Prolonged Exposure (PE) therapy.

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP): Patients are exposed to obsessive thoughts but prevented from performing compulsions.

Phobias

For example, fear of flying, spiders, or heights.

Panic Disorder

Exposure to physical sensations associated with panic (like racing heart) to reduce fear of those sensations.

Social Anxiety Disorder

Exposure to feared social situations like public speaking or initiating conversation.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Less commonly used, but sometimes integrated with CBT techniques.

Types of Exposure

Type

Description

In Vivo Exposure

Real-life exposure to feared situations (e.g., touching a doorknob if you have germ phobia).

Imaginal Exposure

Vividly imagining the feared situation or memory (commonly used in PTSD).

Interoceptive Exposure

Inducing physical sensations (e.g., dizziness, rapid heartbeat) to reduce fear of those sensations (used in panic disorder).

Virtual Reality Exposure

Using VR to simulate environments (e.g., for fear of flying or combat-related PTSD).

How It Works (The Psychology)

Based on the principles of habituation, extinction learning, and cognitive restructuring.

Repeated exposure helps reduce anxiety over time because the brain learns the feared outcome doesn’t happen or is manageable.

Also helps people develop coping skills and tolerate uncertainty

iii).Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT)

is a type of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) that was originally developed by Dr. Marsha Linehan in the late 1980s to treat individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD) and chronic suicidality. Since then, it's been adapted for a wide range of mental health issues

DBT combines:

Cognitive-behavioral techniques (to change unhelpful thinking and behaviors)

Mindfulness practices (from Zen Buddhism)

A dialectical approach: balancing acceptance of yourself as you are, with commitment to change

Core Principle is the “Dialectic”

The word dialectical refers to the idea of holding two seemingly opposite things as true at the same time — for example:

“I am doing the best I can” and

“I need to try harder to change.”

This balance between acceptance and change is the foundation of DBT.

The four core principles of DBT

Mindfulness

Teaches you to observe, describe, and participate in the present moment.

Helps you become more aware of thoughts and feelings without being overwhelmed by them.

Distress Tolerance

Teaches you how to cope with crises and emotional pain without making the situation worse.

Includes skills like: Self-soothing, Distraction, Radical acceptance and Improving the moment

Emotion Regulation

Helps you understand and manage intense emotions.

Skills include:

Identifying and labeling emotions

Reducing emotional vulnerability

Changing unwanted emotional responses

Interpersonal Effectiveness

Focuses on how to:

Ask for what you need

Set healthy boundaries

Maintain relationships

Build self-respect in interactions

Psychiatrists, psychologists, psychotherapists, and some primary care physicians carry out these treatments. Therapy may help individuals to understand the cause of their mental health condition. Working with a healthcare professional may help people manage their day-to-day living and reduce any risk of isolation and self-harm.

Medication

Some people take prescribed medications, such as antidepressants, antipsychotics, and drugs for anxiety.

Although these medications cannot cure mental health disorders, some can help improve symptoms. They may also help a person to manage their social interactions and routines.

Some medications boost the body’s absorption of feel-good chemicals, such as serotonin, from the brain.

Other drugs either boost the levels of these chemicals or help prevent their degradation or destruction.

Self-help

A person coping with a mental health condition may decide to make certain lifestyle changes to help them manage their well-being.

Changes may include:

reducing alcohol intake, if applicable

trying to improve sleep quality

eating a balanced, nutritious diet

taking time away from work/intense study, if this is possible

practicing relaxation techniques, such as deep breathing, meditation, and mindfulness

A support network, whether through self-help groups or close friends and family, can be helpful to a person’s well-being.

Outlook

While mental health disorders are common, they vary in severity. Many people can manage their symptoms and lead full lives with treatment and access to support.

For others, recovery may include learning new ways to help manage their lives.

The prevalence of mental health disorders tends to peak in people ages 18–25 but drops significantly among people ages 50 and over.

Having a mental health condition, especially depression, is associated with severe chronic health conditions such as diabetes, stroke, cardiovascular disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

Steps to help maintain mental well-being

The following steps can help a person manage their mental health by reducing their risk of illness, increasing energy levels, and controlling stress:

Regular exercise: Exercising for 45 minutes three to five times a week can help improve a person’s mental health.

Eat a balanced diet and stay hydrated: This can help a person maintain a constant supply of energy throughout the day.

Aim for good-quality sleep: A 2021 review found that significant improvements in sleep quality led to greater improvements in mental health.

Perform relaxing activities: Breathing exercises, meditation, wellness apps, and journaling can help reduce stress and improve overall health and well-being.

Practice mindfulness and gratitude: A person can identify and note things they are grateful for at the end of each day.

Look for positive social interactions: Connecting with people and maintaining meaningful relationships can help reduce stress and be a source of support and practical help in times of need.

Role of regular Exercise in maintaining Mental Well-Being

Regular physical exercise is one of the most powerful natural tools for maintaining and improving mental well-being. It's not just about getting physically fit — it's also about creating chemical, emotional, and psychological benefits that protect your mental health.

  1. Boosts Mood and Reduces Depression

Exercise increases "feel-good" brain chemicals like:

Endorphins (natural painkillers and mood elevators)

Serotonin (linked to mood regulation and happiness)

Dopamine (involved in motivation and pleasure)

Regular physical activity is shown to be as effective as antidepressants in some cases of mild to moderate depression.

  1. Reduces Anxiety and Stress

Exercise reduces levels of stress hormones like cortisol.

It improves your ability to manage anxiety through:

Distraction from worries

Relaxation of muscle tension

Building resilience through facing physical challenges

  1. Improves Sleep Quality

Physical activity helps you fall asleep faster and sleep more deeply, which is vital for emotional regulation and mental clarity.

Better sleep = better mood, focus, and stress tolerance.

  1. Enhances Brain Function

Exercise increases blood flow to the brain, improving:

Memory

Concentration

Executive function (planning, organizing, decision-making)

It also stimulates neurogenesis (growth of new brain cells), especially in the hippocampus, a region linked to emotion and memory.

  1. Boosts Self-Esteem and Confidence

Achieving fitness goals or simply being active can make you feel more capable and in control.

Improves body image and confidence, even without major physical changes.

  1. Increases Social Connection

Group exercise (like team sports, gym classes, or walking groups) provides social interaction, which is crucial for mental health.

Reduces loneliness and can lead to supportive relationships.

  1. Supports Routine and Structure

Having a regular exercise routine helps create predictability, which is comforting during stressful or uncertain times.

Helps anchor your day, giving it a sense of rhythm and purpose.

Additional reading

What is post-traumatic stress disorder or PTSD?

PTSD is a mental health condition that can develop following a traumatic event. People with PTSD have intense, distressing thoughts related to the event and may have flashbacks and nightmares.

Fear and shock are natural responses to trauma, and most people recover from these with time. If anxiety and other negative feelings continue, doctors may diagnose PTSD.

PTSD can disrupt a person’s life for years, but treatment can help them recover.

What are traumatic events?

Examples of events that can trigger PTSD include accidents, wars, crimes, fires, the death of a loved one, abuse, or any event that causes shock and fear.

Thoughts and memories may recur even though the danger has passed.

Types of PTSD

Mental health professionals may diagnose various subtypes of PTSD. These subtypes may have different underlying causes and symptoms. Subtypes include:

Complex PTSD: This may develop following repeated trauma, particularly in childhood.

Dissociative PTSD (D-PTSD): People with D-PTSD may experience dissociative symptoms, such as feeling that they or the world around them are not real.

Birth trauma: This can occur following a traumatic childbirth experience.

Delayed expression PTSD: People with this subtype experience symptoms 6 months or more after a traumatic event.

PTSD for children 6 years and younger: This subtype recognizes the specific signs of PTSD that young children may express.

Only D-PTSD and the preschool subtype are included in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition (DSM-5). Psychiatrists use the DSM-5 to help diagnose mental health conditions.

Symptoms of PTSD in adults

Symptoms usually start within 3 months  of the traumatic event, but they can begin later.

For a person to receive a diagnosis of PTSD, they must meet criteria set out in the DSM-5.

According to these guidelines, the person must:

experience exposure to death or a personal death threat, a serious injury or sexual violence whether directly, as a witness, by it happening to a loved one, or during professional duties

experience the following for more than 1 month:

one or more intrusion symptoms

one or more avoidance symptoms

two or more symptoms that affect mood and thinking

two or more arousal and reactivity symptoms that began after the trauma

Below are examples of these types of symptoms:

Intrusion symptoms: include:

nightmares

flashbacks and a sensation that the event is happening again

fearful thoughts

Avoidance symptoms: include:

refusing to discuss the event

avoiding situations that remind the person of the event

Symptoms that affect mood and thinking

These include:

inability to remember some aspects of the event

feelings of guilt and blame

feeling detached and estranged from others and emotionally and mentally numbed

having reduced interest in life

difficulty concentrating

mental health problems, such as depression, phobias, and anxiety

Arousal and reactivity symptoms: include:

difficulty sleeping

irritability and angry outbursts

hypersensitivity to possible dangers

feeling tense and anxious

In addition, these symptoms must cause a person distress or difficulty coping with work or relationships. The symptoms must not be due to the use of medications or other substances or another health condition.

Physical symptoms of PTSD

People may also have the following physical symptoms of PTSD, but the DSM-5 criteria do not include these:

physical effects, including sweating, shaking, headaches, stomach problems, dizziness, aches and pains, and chest pain

a weakened immune system, which can lead to more frequent infections

sleep disturbances that can result in tiredness and other problems

A person may experience long-term behavioral changes that contribute to problems at work and a breakdown in their relationships. They may start to seek numbing behaviors, such as misusing alcohol, drugs, or medications.

In severe cases, PTSD may cause anger outbursts that make it difficult for the person to control. This would create a barrier in maintaining a relationship in which both parties feel safe. In instances where trauma is due to sexual violence, it might be particularly difficult to establish healthy boundaries and expectations in regards to intimacy.

—Andy Watkins-McCall, MD, MPH

Symptoms of PTSD in children and teens

In children, symptoms of PTSD may include:

bed wetting after learning to use the bathroom

an inability to speak

acting out the event in their play

being clingy with an adult

Children may not have flashbacks, but be able to remember the traumatic event clearly. However, they may remember it in a different order or feel that there was a sign that it was going to happen.

Children may also act out or express their trauma through their play, drawings, and stories. They may have nightmares, act irritable, and find it hard to go to school, study, or spend time with friends.

Starting from 8 years of age, children’s reactions are generally similar to adults.

Between the ages of 12 and 18 years, the child may show disruptive, disrespectful, impulsive, or aggressive behavior.

They may feel guilty for not acting differently during the event, or they may consider revenge.

Children who have experienced sexual abuse are more likely to:

feel fear, sadness, anxiety, and isolation

have low self-esteem

behave in an aggressive manner

display unusual sexual behavior

hurt themselves

misuse drugs or alcohol

Causes and Risk Factors of PTSD

PTSD can develop after any traumatic event. Any situation that triggers fear, shock, horror, or helplessness can lead to PTSD.

Examples include:

military service, natural disasters, serious accidents, terrorist attacks, loss of a loved one, whether or not this involved violence, rape or other types of abuse, personal assault, being a victim of crime and receiving a life-threatening diagnosis etc.

It is unclear why some people develop PTSD while others do not. However, the following risk factors may increase the chances of a person experiencing symptoms:

having additional problems after an event, for example, losing a loved one and losing a job

lacking social support after an event

having a history of mental health problems or substance misuse

experience of abuse, for example, during childhood

getting a physical injury, possibly as a result of the event

What reduces the risk?

Scientists are looking at resilience factors that may help people recover from or avoid PTSD more effectively. These include:

seeking out and receiving support from others

having or developing positive coping strategies

being able to feel good about their actions when facing difficulty
